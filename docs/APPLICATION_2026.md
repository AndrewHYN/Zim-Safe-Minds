# Zimbabwe Youth Innovation HackLab 2026 — application working draft

**Project:** Zim Safe Minds  
**Tagline:** A safe space to talk. A path to real support.  
**One category only:** Category 1 — Holistic Youth Health and Well-being  
**Application due:** 20 October 2026, 23:59 CAT  
**Applicant status:** Early prototype. No completed clinical pilot, clinical partner agreement, provider directory, AI service or award is claimed.

## Validated root causes selected from the official call
1. **Social, cultural and behavioural barriers:** stigma and norms discourage health-seeking among some young people.
2. **Health system and service delivery gaps:** lack of youth-friendly pathways to appropriate care.
3. **Information and digital access gaps:** confusion about where credible, affordable services can be reached; barriers for lower-data and disabled users.

The application should make the **first root cause** the primary one, with the second and third clearly linked; all remain within **Category 1**. The official call states that mental ill-health is an effect, *not* itself a root cause.

## Problem–solution summary (under 200 words)

For some young adults in Zimbabwe, stigma and discomfort with traditional help-seeking make the first step toward mental-health care difficult. Even when support exists, knowing whom to trust, what it costs, how to connect, and what must be disclosed can be confusing. This directly addresses Category 1's validated root causes: **social and cultural barriers to youth health-seeking**, together with gaps in youth-responsive services and accessible information.

**Zim Safe Minds** is a youth-led, low-data digital bridge from private emotional expression to real support. A young person can begin with optional guided reflection, explore wellbeing resources, then use a simple care navigator to choose preferences such as language, affordability and online versus face-to-face contact. The eventual pilot would connect consenting users to independently verified service partners, with no automatic sharing of private conversations.

We will co-design the pathway with young adults and qualified Zimbabwean mental-health professionals. A 12-month pilot will test whether users can identify an appropriate service and complete a human-support connection; we will also measure safety, accessibility and privacy concerns. The current working prototype demonstrates the journey, **not live therapy, AI triage or active referrals**.

## Innovation description (under 500 words)

**The problem:** A young person may know they are struggling but still hesitate to approach a clinic or professional. Stigma, uncertainty about trustworthy support, money, language and fear of revealing personal details can all create friction between recognizing a need and actually reaching help. Zim Safe Minds proposes to reduce this friction, not replace existing mental-health services.

**What the innovation does:** The first experience is a simple, private, youth-designed interface that can help someone put feelings into words without pressure. From any point in the journey, the user can skip directly to human-support information. A choice-based navigator makes care preferences explicit—online or in person, language and affordability—without a diagnosis, risk score or mandatory disclosure. Users can copy a minimal preference note but no chat transcript is forwarded. In the envisioned pilot, verified providers or established services would receive introduction requests only after explicit user consent, while the user sees transparent information about services, accessibility and costs.

**Why this approach:** Zimbabwe already has important initiatives including Friendship Bench's youth and WhatsApp-based services. We will not reproduce their proven counseling model or claim to be their partner. Our proposed point of innovation is a neutral, low-data **bridge across the help-seeking journey**, emphasizing choice, provider-verification, understandable service information, privacy and follow-through. We will co-design and validate whether that bridge meets an unmet need before scaling.

**Build and test:** We have built an interactive React/TypeScript prototype containing guided, scripted conversations, optional grounding, an urgent-help route, and a care navigator that links to an independent publicly published support service. It presently contains no real AI, appointments or user database. First, youth researchers and clinical advisors will validate access barriers and safeguarding rules. Next, we will verify consenting providers, complete legal and privacy review, and develop the minimum secure consent-based referral workflow. A small pilot involving approximately 50 consenting young adults aged 18–35 and ideally two local service partners will run only after required approvals. Proposed measures are successful identification of suitable care options, completed partner contacts when confirmed by consent, time to human contact, affordability, inclusion and adverse events. Targets are **proposed**, not reported achievements.

**Scale and sustainability:** If evidence supports the model, we would adapt the service directory and referral workflow to other districts and languages, integrating with existing community and clinical providers. Future support could come from institution-sponsored care-navigation arrangements, not selling sensitive conversations, targeted advertising or paywalls on urgent support. Any AI-assisted writing or supportive chat would be optional and added only following clinical evaluation, independent safety review, secure architecture and user consent.

## Youth leadership & inclusion (customize before submitting)

The concept and source code are youth-owned and developed in Zimbabwe. Applicants should list only actual eligible team members and their real roles. Recruitment of a team of 2–5, with at least 40% female participation, is encouraged under the official call; do not claim such a team before it exists. Pilot co-design should include young women and men, youth with disabilities, low-bandwidth Android users, and participants with varying language and affordability needs. An accessible no-chat route will always allow people to reach human-service information directly.
