## Primary root cause
**Social, cultural and behavioural barriers**, especially stigma and negative norms discouraging youth from seeking mental-health services, as named in the official Category 1 list. Information and youth-friendly delivery gaps are secondary contributing barriers, not separate competition categories.

## Problem–solution summary (187 words, limit 200)

Some young Zimbabweans delay seeking mental-health support because stigma, fear of judgment, and uncertainty about affordable, youth-friendly services make the first contact difficult. This targets Category 1's validated root cause: social, cultural, and behavioural barriers that prevent young people from accessing health services. Poor mental health is an effect, not the root cause we claim to solve.

Zim Safe Minds is a youth-led, low-data bridge from private self-expression to credible human care. People can begin with optional guided reflection or skip directly to care options. A navigator collects only contact-format, affordability and language preferences and creates a neutral first-contact message that users decide whether to send. It never automatically shares their chat transcript. Existing independent public support is linked transparently; the future pilot will add consent-based introductions to verified providers.

Our React prototype demonstrates this pathway; the chat is currently scripted, with no live AI therapy, diagnosis or referrals. With US$5,000 we propose youth and clinician co-design, a privacy and accessibility review, provider verification and a small controlled adult pilot. Success will mean young people actually reaching credible human support, not merely spending time in a chatbot.

## Innovation description (405 words, limit 500)

**The barrier.** Although mental-health support already exists in Zimbabwe, some youth hesitate to seek it because of stigma, discomfort disclosing intimate feelings, uncertain fees and unfamiliar service pathways. This is the HackLab Category 1 root cause of social, cultural and behavioural barriers. The question we will test is whether a private, nonjudgmental first step makes reaching real care easier.

**The solution.** Zim Safe Minds is a mobile-first care navigation platform with optional AI-guided emotional expression. A visitor can begin with supportive reflection or bypass it entirely. They can explore an optional grounding activity, select language, price and contact preferences, then view publicly described services and create a neutral introductory message they can independently review, copy and send. The prototype does not ask for a diagnosis or share a chat transcript. The live demo links to Friendship Bench's independent public service without suggesting a partnership.

**The difference.** We are not claiming to invent chat-based mental-health assistance: Zimbabwe already has Friendship Bench and other care providers. Our proposed innovation is a consent-controlled bridge between wanting support and making an actual first human contact, with clear independent service information, verifiable providers, and measurable referral follow-through. We will test whether that gap is genuine rather than duplicate an existing service.

**The role of AI.** AI-assisted reflection remains a major product goal, but it must never make clinical decisions. Our present demo uses scripted responses, not a live model. Any later AI would be developed with Zimbabwean clinicians, informed consent, secure data handling, evaluation across local language needs, and clear escalation boundaries. It will not diagnose, prescribe, autonomously triage crisis risk or choose treatment.

**The 12-month pilot.** Months 1–2: nonclinical youth interviews and professional consultations. Months 3–4: clinical safeguarding, legal, privacy and disability-access design. Months 5–6: verify willing providers and build a secure opt-in referral workflow. Months 7–8: with approvals, pilot with up to 50 consenting adults aged 18–35 and seek two verified partners. Months 9–10: evaluate time to contact, successful connections, barriers, user experience and safety incidents. Months 11–12: iterate, report and decide whether to scale, pivot or stop.

**Feasibility, inclusion and viability.** The US$5,000 grant funds a narrow pilot, not a nationwide medical-AI business. The user pathway should remain free. We will explore transparent institutional sponsorship only after evaluating benefits, privacy and conflicts of interest. Low-data Android access, gender-sensitive care, disability inclusion and Shona/Ndebele language preferences are built into the research plan. No sensitive data will be sold.

## Target users
Consenting youth aged 18–35 ordinarily resident in Zimbabwe, initially Harare and nearby communities, who face stigma, uncertainty about services or affordability barriers when seeking mental-health support. The eventual long-term product may serve everyone; this seed-funded pilot focuses on this eligible youth group.

## Inclusion, gender, disability and SRHR (101 words)
The first pilot will include young women and men, youth with disabilities, and people using entry-level Android phones or limited data. A user can reach human-support information without using AI or revealing intimate details. We will test screen-reader and keyboard access, clear language, affordability information, and Shona/Ndebele preferences (without falsely claiming full translation today). Gender equity and SRHR matter because stigma or fear of judgment can particularly affect people seeking reproductive-health or trauma-related help. Clinical advisers will help develop appropriate trauma-informed, consent-based pathways before any such referral is introduced. We will not collect SRHR or mental-health histories in open demo forms.
